A Tragedy in Five Acts

PROLOGUE

The Night Before

There are things a man can lose and recover.

Money can be earned again.

A house can be rebuilt.

A reputation, though difficult, can sometimes be restored.

Even a broken friendship may find its way back to forgiveness.

But there are things that do not return once they are gone.

Childhood is one of them.

A father knows this.

Or at least, he should.

Yet there are fathers who leave their children while they are still small enough to believe that a parent's absence must have an explanation.

There are fathers who remain in the same house and are still absent.

There are fathers who provide everything except themselves.

And there are fathers who love their children deeply, but whose love is buried beneath pride, fear, anger, addiction, shame, or wounds they never learned how to name.

This is the story of one such father.

His name was Samuel.

He was not born cruel.

That is important.

He was not born knowing how to abandon people.

He was once a boy himself.

Someone had once held him.

Someone had once called him son.

Someone had once promised him that the world would be different.

But promises have a strange way of becoming memories.

And memories have a strange way of becoming patterns.

Samuel would spend his life running from the man who raised him.

He did not know that sometimes, the thing we run from learns how to run inside us.

ACT I

THE HOUSE

Chapter One

The Table

The first thing Daniel remembered about his father was not his face.

It was the sound of his spoon.

Every evening, Samuel sat at the head of the table with the same old wooden spoon in his right hand. It had belonged to his own father, though Daniel would not learn that until many years later.

The spoon struck the enamel plate.

Clink.

A pause.

Clink.

Another pause.

Daniel would sit opposite him, his small legs swinging beneath the chair because they could not yet reach the floor.

He watched everything.

Children do that.

Adults think children are playing.

They are often studying the room.

Daniel studied his father.

He studied the way Samuel removed his shoes before entering the house.

The way he cleared his throat before speaking.

The way his eyes changed whenever Mother mentioned money.

The way he became quiet whenever somebody asked about his past.

And most of all, he studied his father's silence.

Samuel was not a man who shouted often.

That was what made his silence frightening.

When he was angry, the house knew.

Not because he said anything.

Because he stopped saying everything.

“Eat,” he would say.

Daniel would eat.

“Go to bed.”

Daniel would go.

“Don't ask questions.”

Daniel stopped asking.

There was an invisible line in the house, and Daniel learned early not to cross it.

One evening, however, he crossed it.

He was seven.

Samuel had returned home late.

His shirt was dusty, his face tired, and there was something in his eyes that Daniel could not understand.

Samuel sat down.

Mother served him food.

He did not say thank you.

Daniel watched him for several minutes.

Then he asked:

“Father?”

Samuel looked up.

“Yes?”

“Are you happy?”

Mother's hand stopped above the table.

Samuel stared at the boy.

Daniel waited.

He had asked the question innocently.

Children do not understand that some questions are dangerous.

Samuel finally smiled.

It was a small smile.

Almost convincing.

“I am tired.”

Daniel nodded.

“Oh.”

And he returned to his food.

But something had changed.

Not in Samuel.

In Daniel.

That night, while lying in bed, he thought about the answer.

I am tired.

It was strange.

His father had not said yes.

He had not said no.

He had simply changed the question.

Daniel did not know it then, but that was the first lesson his father taught him about adults:

Sometimes people answer the question they wish you had asked.

Chapter Two

The Things Children Notice

Daniel's mother believed children should not know too much.

“Let adults handle adult problems,” she often said.

So Daniel learned to become invisible whenever his parents spoke.

He would sit in the corridor with a book he was not reading.

He would pretend to sleep.

He would listen through walls.

Not because he wanted secrets.

Because secrets had begun to shape his home.

There were nights when his parents argued.

There were nights when they whispered.

And there were nights when there was no sound at all.

Those were the nights Daniel feared most.

Because shouting meant people were still fighting for something.

Silence meant somebody had already given up.

One night, Daniel woke to the sound of his mother's voice.

Very low.

Almost a whisper.

“You cannot keep doing this.”

Samuel answered.

“I know.”

“Then change.”

“I am trying.”

“No, Samuel. You're surviving. That's different.”

Silence.

Daniel remained still beneath his blanket.

He heard a chair move.

Then his mother said:

“What are you running from?”

Nothing.

Then:

“Your father?”

Daniel opened his eyes.

His father?

Samuel did not answer.

His mother continued.

“You think Daniel doesn't see it.”

Another silence.

“He sees everything.”

Daniel pulled the blanket over his head.

He did not want to hear anymore.

But it was already too late.

He had discovered something that night.

His father was afraid of something.

And whatever that something was, Daniel's mother knew its name.

Chapter Three

The Door

The night Samuel left, there was no thunder.

No rain.

No dramatic farewell.

That is how Daniel remembered it.

The world did not stop to announce that something important was happening.

A neighbour's radio played somewhere down the road.

A dog barked twice.

A motorcycle passed.

Inside the house, Samuel packed a small bag.

Daniel stood in the doorway.

“Where are you going?”

Samuel stopped.

He looked at his son.

For several seconds, neither spoke.

Then Samuel walked toward him.

He knelt.

Daniel looked into his father's eyes.

“Are you coming back?”

Samuel swallowed.

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Soon.”

Daniel nodded.

That word was enough.

Soon.

Samuel placed his hand on the boy's shoulder.

Then he stood.

He walked past him.

Opened the door.

And left.

Daniel ran to the window.

He watched his father walk down the road.

One hundred metres.

Two hundred.

Then the darkness swallowed him.

Daniel waited.

He expected Samuel to turn around.

He didn't.

That was the moment Daniel learned that a person could disappear without dying.

Chapter Four

The Empty Chair

The next morning, Daniel woke before his mother.

For a few seconds, he forgot.

He opened his eyes and stared at the ceiling.

Then he remembered the door.

He climbed out of bed.

The house looked exactly as it had the night before.

His father's shoes were gone.

His jacket was gone.

The small bag he had packed was gone.

But the chair at the table remained.

Daniel stood beside it.

He touched the back of the chair with both hands.

It was strange that an empty chair could look so much like a person.

He pulled it out.

Then he pushed it back.

He did this twice.

On the third time, he sat in it.

It was his father's chair.

He knew he was not supposed to sit there.

Still, he did.

His feet barely touched the floor.

He looked toward the doorway.

Perhaps his father would walk in.

Perhaps this was the morning he had meant when he said soon.

Daniel waited.

His mother eventually came into the room.

She stopped when she saw him.

“Daniel.”

He looked up.

“I'm waiting for Father.”

His mother said nothing.

“He said he would come back.”

“I know.”

“Today?”

She walked toward the stove.

“Eat your breakfast.”

“Mother.”

“Daniel.”

Her voice was gentle, but firm.

“Eat.”

He looked at the empty doorway one last time.

Then he climbed down from the chair.

He never sat in it again.

For years.

Chapter Five

A House Can Become Smaller

People think a house becomes smaller when walls are removed.

They are wrong.

A house becomes smaller when someone leaves.

After Samuel went away, nothing physically changed.

The same roof remained above them.

The same windows opened toward the road.

The same table stood in the kitchen.

The same plates were washed every evening.

But somehow, there was less room.

His mother began doing things differently.

She stopped cooking Samuel's favourite meals.

She stopped leaving the porch light on.

She stopped looking toward the road whenever a motorcycle passed.

And she stopped saying his name.

Daniel noticed.

He noticed everything.

One afternoon, while his mother folded clothes, he asked:

“Mother?”

“Yes?”

“Did Father leave because of me?”

Her hands stopped.

She looked at him.

“No.”

“Did I do something?”

“No.”

“Then why did he go?”

His mother folded the shirt again.

“He had his reasons.”

“What reasons?”

She looked toward the window.

“Some things are difficult to explain.”

Daniel hated that sentence.

It sounded like an answer.

But it wasn't.

That evening, he went outside and sat beneath the old mango tree.

He watched people walking along the road.

Every man in the distance looked like his father for half a second.

Then the distance corrected him.

Not him.

Not him.

Not him.

Daniel began to understand that waiting could become a habit.

Chapter Six

The Man Who Stayed

If Samuel was the man who left, then Daniel's mother was the woman who stayed.

But staying was not the same as being alright.

She worked harder.

She spoke less.

She became good at stretching small amounts of money across long weeks.

She learned how to repair things she had never repaired before.

She learned how to answer questions from neighbours without actually answering them.

“Where is Samuel?”

“He's away.”

“For how long?”

“I don't know.”

“Are you two still together?”

She would smile.

“We are fine.”

She became an expert at saying fine.

Daniel learned from her.

Soon, the house had two people who were both pretending.

One pretended she was not hurt.

The other pretended he was not waiting.

At night, Daniel sometimes heard his mother crying.

She never cried loudly.

She cried the way people do when they do not want their grief to disturb anyone.

Daniel would lie in his bed and listen.

He wanted to go to her.

He wanted to tell her that he was awake.

But something stopped him.

Perhaps he thought she would be embarrassed.

Perhaps he thought he was supposed to be strong.

Or perhaps he had already begun learning the lesson his father had left behind:

Keep things inside.

So Daniel stayed in bed.

And his mother cried alone.

Two lonely people slept beneath the same roof.

Neither knew how to reach the other.

Chapter Seven

Father's Day

Years later, Daniel would remember one particular day.

At school, the teacher told the children to write a letter to their fathers.

Daniel stared at the blank page.

Around him, children began writing.

Some wrote about football.

Some wrote about the things their fathers bought them.

One boy wrote that his father was his hero.

Daniel held his pencil.

The teacher walked between the desks.

“Come on, Daniel.”

He looked up.

“Write something.”

“What should I write?”

“Anything you want to tell your father.”

Daniel looked at the page.

Then he wrote:

Dear Father,

He stopped.

What came next?

He had forgotten his father's handwriting.

He had not forgotten his face.

But handwriting was different.

Handwriting belonged to someone who stayed long enough to leave traces.

Daniel stared at the words.

Then he crossed them out.

He tried again.

Dear Dad,

Nothing.

He turned the paper over.

On the other side, he wrote:

Where are you?

He looked at the sentence.

Then he tore the page into pieces.

The teacher found him after class.

“Daniel, where is your letter?”

“I lost it.”

The teacher frowned.

“Lost it?”

“Yes.”

She handed him another sheet.

“Try again.”

Daniel took it.

That evening, he brought the blank paper home.

His mother found it in his schoolbag.

She looked at him.

“You didn't write to him?”

“No.”

“Why?”

Daniel shrugged.

She sat beside him.

For a moment, she seemed about to say something.

Instead, she placed the paper back into his bag.

“Maybe another time.”

Daniel nodded.

Another time.

Another word that sounded a lot like soon.

Chapter Eight

The First Lie

The first time Daniel lied about his father, he was eleven.

A boy at school asked:

“Where does your father work?”

Daniel knew the truth.

He could have said:

He left.

Instead, he said:

“He works in another town.”

The boy nodded.

“What does he do?”

Daniel hesitated.

“Construction.”

It came easily.

Too easily.

By the end of the week, Daniel had given his father a job, a workplace, and a town.

By the end of the month, he had invented an entire life for him.

His father travelled.

His father worked hard.

His father was always busy.

His father would come home one day.

The lie protected him.

At school, he was no longer the boy whose father had disappeared.

He was the boy whose father was away working.

There was dignity in that story.

There was hope in it.

And Daniel discovered something dangerous:

Sometimes a lie hurts less than the truth.

But every lie creates a room inside you.

And if you live inside it long enough, you eventually forget where the door is.

Chapter Nine

The Road

Daniel grew older.

The road outside their house remained the same.

Dust in the dry season.

Mud in the rain.

Motorcycles passing in the morning.

People returning home in the evening.

Every sound carried a possibility.

Whenever a vehicle slowed near the house, Daniel looked up.

Whenever footsteps approached the gate, his heart moved before his mind could stop it.

He never admitted this to anyone.

Not even himself.

Then one afternoon, a black car stopped outside.

Daniel was sixteen.

He was doing homework beneath the mango tree.

The door opened.

A man stepped out.

For one impossible second, Daniel thought:

Father.

His heart raced.

The man turned.

It wasn't him.

Daniel looked back at his book.

He forced himself to continue reading.

The man entered the neighbour's house.

Daniel stared at the same line for nearly ten minutes.

He could not remember a single word.

That evening, he understood something he had never wanted to understand.

He was no longer waiting for his father to return.

He was waiting for the waiting to end.

And those are not the same thing.

Chapter Ten

A Boy Becomes a Man

At sixteen, Daniel stopped waiting by the window.

Not because he had stopped hoping.

Because he had learned to hide it.

There is a certain age when boys begin to understand that the world expects something from them.

Nobody sits them down and explains it.

It simply arrives.

Their voices deepen.

Their shoulders widen.

People begin calling them young men.

And suddenly, things that were once forgiven because they were children are no longer forgiven.

Daniel felt it happening.

His mother began sending him to negotiate things.

“Go and speak to the landlord.”

“Go and ask them when the money will be ready.”

“Go and tell your uncle we need help.”

He noticed something.

Whenever adults saw him, they no longer looked for a child.

They looked for the man he was supposed to become.

Daniel didn't know how to become that man.

So he built one.

He decided that a man should not complain.

A man should not cry.

A man should not ask for help.

A man should solve his own problems.

A man should never allow anyone to see that he is afraid.

He did not invent these rules.

He inherited them.

Chapter Eleven

The First Fight

It happened behind the school.

Daniel was walking home when he heard laughter.

Three boys were standing near the fence.

One of them called his name.

“Daniel.”

He stopped.

“What?”

The boy smiled.

“We heard your father ran away.”

The laughter started.

Daniel said nothing.

Another boy stepped closer.

“Is it true?”

Daniel looked at him.

“My father is working.”

“Working where?”

“Somewhere.”

“Then why doesn't he come home?”

Daniel clenched his fists.

The boys laughed again.

One of them said:

“Maybe he doesn't want you.”

That sentence changed everything.

Daniel moved before he had thought about it.

The fight lasted less than a minute.

By the time a teacher found them, Daniel's shirt was torn and his knuckles were sore.

The teacher took him to the office.

“What happened?”

Daniel said nothing.

“Daniel.”

Nothing.

“Why did you fight him?”

Daniel looked at the floor.

“He talked about my father.”

The teacher sighed.

“You cannot fight people every time they say something you don't like.”

Daniel finally looked up.

“What if they are right?”

The teacher had no answer.

Chapter Twelve

The Man in the Photograph

His mother kept one photograph.

It was hidden inside an old book in the bedroom.

Daniel discovered it by accident.

He was looking for a school certificate when the photograph slipped from between the pages.

He picked it up.

His father was young.

Much younger than Daniel had ever imagined.

Samuel was standing beside another man.

An older man.

Both were smiling.

Daniel turned the photograph over.

There was writing on the back.

Samuel and Father — 1989.

Daniel stared at it.

Father.

Not my father.

His father's father.

His grandfather.

Daniel had never seen him.

He carried the photograph into the kitchen.

“Mother?”

She looked up.

“Where did you get that?”

“Who is this?”

She didn't answer.

“Mother.”

She took the photograph from his hand.

For a moment, something changed in her face.

Fear.

Or sadness.

Perhaps both.

“Where did Father get this?”

“He kept it.”

“Who is the man?”

She looked at the photograph.

“Your grandfather.”

Daniel frowned.

“You knew him?”

“Yes.”

“What was he like?”

His mother returned the photograph to the book.

“You should not worry about things that happened before you were born.”

Daniel stared at her.

“Why?”

“Because they cannot be changed.”

That answer stayed with him.

Not because it satisfied him.

Because it didn't.

Chapter Thirteen

The Name Nobody Spoke

Daniel began asking questions.

Quietly.

Not to his mother.

To people who had known Samuel before he became his father.

An old neighbour remembered him.

“He was different when he was young.”

“How?”

The old man laughed.

“Your father? He used to talk too much.”

Daniel smiled.

That was difficult to imagine.

“He wanted to leave this place,” the man continued.

“Why?”

“He hated his father.”

Daniel's smile disappeared.

“What did his father do?”

The old man looked at him.

“Who told you that?”

“Nobody.”

“Then leave it alone.”

Daniel had heard those words before.

Leave it alone.

Adults loved that sentence.

It was what they said when the truth had teeth.

Daniel asked another person.

Then another.

Each gave him a different piece.

His grandfather was a hard man.

He drank.

He was feared.

He demanded obedience.

He believed children should be grateful for food and shelter and ask for nothing more.

Samuel had grown up under him.

And one day, Samuel had sworn:

“I will never become my father.”

Daniel carried that sentence home.

That night, he looked at himself in the mirror.

For the first time, he wondered:

What if Father didn't leave because he didn't love me?

Then another thought came.

What if he left because he was afraid of himself?

Daniel did not know which possibility was worse.

Chapter Fourteen

The Rules

By eighteen, Daniel had become difficult to read.

He rarely spoke about himself.

When something hurt him, he became quiet.

When he was angry, he walked away.

When someone offered help, he refused.

When he failed, he blamed himself.

When he succeeded, he said it was nothing.

His mother watched him becoming harder.

One evening, she said:

“You don't have to carry everything alone.”

Daniel continued eating.

“I can manage.”

“You are still my son.”

“I know.”

“You don't have to be strong all the time.”

Daniel looked at her.

“Who will be strong if I'm not?”

His mother had no answer.

He returned to his food.

She watched him.

And in that moment, she saw something that frightened her.

She saw Samuel.

Not his face.

His silence.

Chapter Fifteen

The Letter

The letter arrived on a Thursday.

There was no warning.

No phone call.

No explanation.

Just an envelope.

Daniel was home when the postman handed it to his mother.

She looked at the name.

Her face changed.

Daniel noticed.

“What is it?”

“Nothing.”

She put it on the table.

Daniel waited.

She did not open it.

“Mother.”

“It's for you.”

“For me?”

She nodded.

Daniel picked it up.

His name was written on the front.

Daniel Samuel.

His hands became still.

He recognized the handwriting.

He had seen it only once before.

On an old school document.

His father's handwriting.

He looked at his mother.

“Where did this come from?”

She said nothing.

Daniel turned the envelope over.

There was no return address.

Only one sentence written on the back.

My son,

Daniel stood there for a long time.

Eighteen years of silence had been broken by two words.

He opened the envelope.

Inside was a single sheet of paper.

He read the first line.

Then stopped.

He read it again.

His mother watched him.

“What does he say?”

Daniel folded the letter.

“Nothing.”

“Daniel.”

He placed it inside his pocket.

“I'm going out.”

“Where?”

He opened the door.

“I don't know.”

And for the first time in eighteen years, Daniel was no longer waiting for his father.

His father was waiting for him.
